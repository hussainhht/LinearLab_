Solving a system by hand means replacing it with simpler systems that have the same solutions. On the augmented matrix, three moves do this. They are the **elementary row operations**.

| Operation | Notation | Effect on the equations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Swap two rows | $R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$ | List the equations in a different order |
| Scale a row by $k \neq 0$ | $R_i \leftarrow kR_i$ | Multiply both sides of one equation by $k$ |
| Add a multiple of another row | $R_i \leftarrow R_i + kR_j$, with $j \neq i$ | Add $k$ times one equation to another |

Some textbooks write the same operations with the arrow the other way, as $kR_1 \to R_1$ or $R_2 + kR_1 \to R_2$. The meaning is identical, and practice mode accepts both.

## Why the solutions do not change

Each operation turns true equations into true equations, so every solution of the old system solves the new one. Each operation can also be **undone** by another row operation:

- a swap is undone by the same swap;
- scaling by $k$ is undone by scaling by $1/k$, which is why $k = 0$ is not allowed;
- adding $kR_j$ is undone by adding $-kR_j$.

Because we can go back, every solution of the new system also solves the old one. The two systems have exactly the same solution set. Matrices related by row operations are called **row equivalent**.

<Note title="Two common slips" tone="warning">

Multiplying a row by $0$ erases an equation, so it can create solutions that were not there. And $R_1 \leftarrow 2R_1 + R_2$ is not a single elementary operation: it scales and adds at once. It still keeps the solution set because $2 \neq 0$, but write it as two steps when showing your work.

</Note>

## One example of each

<Example title="Swap: R₂ ↔ R₃">

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{\ R_2 \leftrightarrow R_3\ }
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The rows trade places; no entry changes value.

</Example>

<Correction>

The earlier version showed the last row after this swap as $[\,0\ \ 0\ \ 6\,]$. A swap only reorders rows, so the zero row stays a zero row.

</Correction>

<Example title="Scale: R₂ ← ¼R₂">

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow{\ R_2 \leftarrow \frac14 R_2\ }
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & \frac14 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]
$$

</Example>

<Example title="Replace: R₂ ← R₂ − 3R₁">

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 7 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]
\xrightarrow{\ R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 3R_1\ }
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array}\right]
$$

Entry by entry: $3 - 3(1) = 0$, $6 - 3(2) = 0$, $1 - 3(0) = 1$, $7 - 3(1) = 4$.

</Example>
