## The transpose

<Definition term="Transpose">

The **transpose** of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is the $n \times m$ matrix $A^T$ whose rows are the columns of $A$:

$$
(A^T)_{ij} = a_{ji}.
$$

</Definition>

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \ (2 \times 3)
\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad
A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \ (3 \times 2)
$$

| Rule | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Double transpose | $(A^T)^T = A$ |
| Sum | $(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T$ |
| Scalar | $(kA)^T = kA^T$ |
| Product | $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$ |

The product rule reverses the order, and it extends to longer products: $(ABC)^T = C^TB^TA^T$.

<Example title="Checking (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ">

With $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$, we found $AB = \begin{bmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{bmatrix}$, so $(AB)^T = \begin{bmatrix} 19 & 43 \\ 22 & 50 \end{bmatrix}$. In the other order,

$$
B^TA^T = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 7 \\ 6 & 8 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 5 + 14 & 15 + 28 \\ 6 + 16 & 18 + 32 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 19 & 43 \\ 22 & 50 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The un-reversed product $A^TB^T = \begin{bmatrix} 23 & 31 \\ 34 & 46 \end{bmatrix}$ is different; it equals $(BA)^T$.

</Example>

## Special matrices

| Type | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zero matrix $0$ | Every entry is 0 | $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| Identity $I_n$ | Square; 1 on the main diagonal, 0 elsewhere | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| Diagonal | Square; zero off the main diagonal | $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| Scalar | Diagonal with equal diagonal entries, $kI$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| Symmetric | $A^T = A$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| Skew-symmetric | $A^T = -A$; the diagonal must be 0 | $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ -2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| Upper triangular | Zero below the main diagonal | $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ |
| Lower triangular | Zero above the main diagonal | $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ |

The identity plays the role of the number 1: $AI = IA = A$. The diagonal of a skew-symmetric matrix must be zero, because $a_{ii} = -a_{ii}$ forces $a_{ii} = 0$.
